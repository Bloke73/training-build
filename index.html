A complete training build documented session by session, starting at age 53 from a standing start — no pull-ups, no plan, just a decision to stop treating the gym as optional. Every session was generated as its own artifact. This page is what 132 of those files add up to when you actually measure them.

Not a program to copy — a method to steal. This is what a real six months looks like from the inside, including the repeated days, the skipped ones, and the parts that had to be rebuilt after they stopped working. Every rule on this page exists because something broke first.

The 42% is the whole story
Nearly half the exercise pool ran exactly once. That isn't randomness — it's the cost of a rule adopted partway through: before calling any movement "new," verify it against the entire history, not the last few sessions. That rule got added because it was broken first. More on that below.

The anchor metric

The Pull-Up
Ladder

One movement got tracked harder than anything else, because it was the honest measure: zero unbroken pull-ups at the start. The long-term target is 25. The realistic interim target, set after doing the math out loud rather than optimistically, is ~12 unbroken by May 2027.

Getting there produced 25 distinct pull-up and hang variants across 123 placements — the most heavily engineered single pattern in the entire build. The ladder, by frequency:

25
Pull-Up — 4-Second Top Hold
The permanent staple. Never rotated out. Time under tension at the hardest position.
17
Scapular Pull-Up
Teaches the initiation most beginners never learn — engaging the back before the arms.
17

Grip and shoulder decompression. Also the fallback on light and recovery days.
15
Negative Pull-Up
Slow eccentrics. The single highest-leverage movement for getting from zero to reps.
6
Straight Pull-Up — Opener Set
Added later and deliberately: a fresh, unfatigued max set logged before any other work, so progress was measured honestly instead of after the tank was empty.
5

Overload on the grip without adding rep volume.
3

The opener set was a correction, not a feature
For months, pull-up maxes were being attempted after volume work — which meant every number was measuring fatigue, not strength. Adding a fresh opener set at the very front of pull days fixed the measurement. If you're tracking a max, test it first or don't call it a max.
The system

Four Day Types,
Five Hard Rules

The structure wasn't designed up front. It accumulated — each rule added the moment something broke. It started as a 3-day rotation and became four when it became obvious that the lagging body parts were never going to catch up on integration work alone.

🪢
Day 1 — Pull
Bodyweight pull strength and bouldering prep. Opens with the fresh max set. Grip work included, since climbing exposed that bar strength and finger strength are genuinely different adaptations.
🏋️
Day 2 — Lift
Heavy loaded strength with core integrated rather than appended. Legs adjusted daily against whatever outdoor activity was planned that afternoon.
🧘
Day 3 — Flex + Core
Flexibility and core reinforcement. Later layered with named yoga poses in addition to standard stretching — never as a replacement — so progress became trackable against a named pose instead of a vague feeling.
🎯
Day 4 — Isolation
Added in month six. Direct work on the spots refusing to progress at the same rate as everything else. Must still carry one movement from each other day so no pattern goes dormant.
🔍
Rule 1 — Verify Against Everything
Never claim a movement is "new" or "rare" by checking recent sessions. Check the entire history. This rule exists because it was violated: five exercises were once presented as fresh when one of them had already run 11 times, and another used equipment that had been explicitly ruled out. Every novelty claim since then carries an actual count.
📦
Rule 2 — Blocks, Not Endless Novelty
Movements are grouped into blocks. Block 1 was the original pool. Block 2 introduced genuinely new movements on a 4-week resurfacing cycle — often enough to build skill, rare enough to stay fresh. Favorites from a retiring block stay on a back burner rather than disappearing.
📌
Rule 3 — Some Things Never Rotate
The 4-second pull-up hold and pigeon pose (right side always held longer, because it needs it) are fixed by design. Rotation is for variety. Staples are for progression. Confusing the two wastes both.
🔗
Rule 4 — Nothing Goes Fully Dormant
Every primary day carries integration touches from the others — a pull movement, a core movement, a balance movement — each distinct from the day's main work and from each other. A pattern skipped for a week is a pattern you lose ground on.
📅
Rule 5 — Never Plan More Than One Day Out
Every session was built the morning of, against what actually happened the day before. Weekly plans were tried and abandoned — they were consistently wrong by day three, because real life includes unplanned hikes, a BMX crash, and bartending shifts.

The actual point

What The Gym Work
Is Actually For

This is the part most training logs leave out, and it's the part that makes the rest make sense. The gym was never the goal. It's 35–40 minutes of maintenance that buys the ability to do these — and every session was built against whatever was happening outside that day.

That's the actual mechanism worth copying: legs stay light before a climb, grip gets prioritized before bouldering, a hard hike replaces the gym rather than stacking on top of it. The training serves the activity, not the reverse.

🚵BMX Hill Climbs
The engine
The most frequent outdoor session — typically 4 to 5 hill climbs, often at sunset. Brutal on legs, lungs and lower back. This is the one that dictates how legs get programmed the next morning more than anything else.
Gym linkLegs kept light the day of · calf and hip flexor work the day after · hard climbs push the next session upper-body
🧗Bouldering
The skill
Outdoors at Eldorado Canyon, plus indoor sessions. A crash pad got bought after one near-miss made the risk obvious. Bouldering is also what exposed the single biggest gap in the program — see the grip note below.
Gym linkGrip work and pull volume front-loaded before climbing days · balance and lateral hip mobility feed edging directly
🥾Hiking
The volume
Front Range trails, the biggest being a 7.3-mile ascent of Twin Sisters. A hike that size is the session — it replaced the gym that day rather than getting added to it, and the next day's work skipped legs entirely.
Gym linkAnkle mobility and calf work pre-loaded · legs excluded the following day · extra calf stretch time after
🏂Snowboarding
The target
The thing the whole back half of the build is aimed at. Season pass targeted for early November, Copper or Winter Park. Once the season starts, gym frequency drops on purpose — riding provides the load, the same way an in-season athlete trains less.
Gym linkAnkle dorsiflexion for edge control · 90/90 hip rotation for stance · thoracic rotation for edge-to-edge transitions
🛹Skateboarding
The carryover
Same balance and edge-control demands as snowboarding, available year-round without a lift ticket. Reactive balance under an unstable surface — which is exactly why Bosu and single-leg work stayed in the rotation all six months.
Gym linkBosu single-leg reach · eyes-closed balance holds · ankle stability work
🏊Swimming
The new one
Newest addition, starting at 10-lap sessions in a 25-yard pool. Zero-impact conditioning that doesn't tax the legs, which makes it the one activity that can stack on a heavy training day instead of competing with it.
Gym linkShoulder mobility and lat stretching support it · no leg cost, so it slots anywhere in the week
The gap bouldering exposed
Pull-up strength does not transfer to climbing the way you'd assume. Hanging off a bar loads the whole hand and recruits lats and biceps. Gripping a thin crimp loads the finger flexor tendons in near-isolation — a genuinely different tissue adaptation. Six months of pull-up work built a strong base and still left a real gap. Dedicated hangboard work is the open item in this program — flagged, understood, deliberately not rushed, because finger tendons are slow to adapt and fast to injure.

Where this goes

Phase Two —
Logging The Outside

Everything measured on this page comes from the gym record, because that's what exists in structured form. The outdoor side — the climbs, the hikes, the rides — has been driving every programming decision for six months without being logged as data.

That's what gets fixed next. Going forward, outdoor activity gets logged the same way sessions were: date, activity, detail, intensity. Once there's a few months of it, the interesting analysis becomes possible — how gym volume and outdoor volume actually trade off, whether the training is genuinely supporting the activities or quietly competing with them, and what happens to both when snowboard season takes over.

Why that matters beyond one person
Plenty of programs tell you what to do in the gym. Very few show the feedback loop — how a hard ride on Tuesday night changes Wednesday morning, and how to make that call yourself. That loop is the transferable part. The exercise list isn't the method; the daily decision is.
If you're starting this at 50+

What Actually
Mattered

Six months in, these are the things that made a measurable difference — and a couple that were learned the expensive way.

Flexibility is not the cooldown
It was the largest single category at 25% of all work, and it's the reason back-to-back training days and outdoor activity were possible at all. Treating it as optional is the fastest way to lose the ability to do the things you're training for.
Put core at the front, not the end
When core was scheduled last it got skipped, reliably. Moving it earlier and integrating it into every session type instead of isolating it onto one day is why it ended up at 23% instead of 5%.
Test your max when you're fresh
Months of pull-up numbers were measuring fatigue because they were attempted after volume work. A fresh opener set at the front of the session is the difference between tracking progress and tracking exhaustion.
Variety needs verification, not vibes
"Let's do something different" produces the same twelve exercises on rotation. Actually checking frequency across the full history — and being willing to be told you already ran that movement eleven times — is what took the pool from a dozen movements to 251.
Build one day at a time
Weekly plans failed consistently. Real life has unplanned 7-mile hikes, a torn-up shin from a bike crash, and on-call shifts. Deciding each morning based on what the body actually did yesterday beat any plan made on Sunday.
Rest days are a decision, not a failure
After six-plus weeks of unbroken consistency, deliberately taking two days off — and building a genuinely light maintenance session instead of a real one — was a periodization choice, not a lapse. At 53, recovery is training.
The gym is not the point
Sessions stayed at 35–40 minutes on purpose. The engagement lives in bouldering, hill climbs, hikes and the upcoming season. The gym exists to make those possible for as long as possible.
Methodology & limitations

What This Data
Does And Doesn't Prove

Stating this plainly, because a progress page that overclaims is worth less than one that doesn't.

Known limitations
  • All counts derive from session artifacts that were generated, not from a verified completion log. A programmed session is strong evidence it happened, not proof.
  • Exact calendar dates aren't recoverable from the files, so the timeline is progression-based rather than date-based. The six-month span runs from the first artifact to the most recent.
  • Some days weren't logged at all. Some sessions were repeated. At least one was built and then replaced by a hike instead.
  • The 5.2 sessions/week average is total documented sessions divided by elapsed weeks. It includes short evening add-on sessions, so it overstates full-length training days.
  • Two different artifact templates were used across the six months (37 early, 87 current) with inconsistent exercise naming. Names were normalized before counting — 257 raw name strings collapsed to 251 distinct movements. A few near-duplicates may still be counted separately.
  • Outdoor activity — the BMX climbs, hikes, bouldering and rides that drove nearly every programming decision — was not logged as structured data during these six months. It's described here but not measured. That starts changing now.
  • No body-composition, load-progression or bodyweight data is presented here, because it wasn't tracked with enough consistency to be honest about.

What it does show reliably: the structure held for six months, the volume was real, and the system got corrected every time it was caught being wrong. For someone deciding whether this is doable starting in their fifties, that's the useful part — not the numbers, but the fact that the thing survived contact with real life for twenty-four straight weeks.
